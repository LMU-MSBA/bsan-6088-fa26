# From the interview to a product brief

We'll interview the office manager together, then use our notes to draft proposals in groups. Your group will explain what it proposes and which interview evidence supports that choice.

A family-law firm wants help preparing printed hearing binders containing documents its attorneys need in court. Staff need the binders ready by 3 pm the previous day. Greg will play the office manager in an anonymous classroom case informed by a current capstone project.

## Interview the office manager

1. Choose a facilitator, a note taker, a listener, and a skeptic.
2. Prepare one question that asks for a recent example.
3. Follow details from the office manager's answers.
4. Ask for a walkthrough of how the work gets done.
5. Reflect back your understanding and listen for corrections.

The facilitator guides the conversation, and the note taker records the account and reflects back. The listener asks a follow-up question. The skeptic asks what we're assuming and checks it with the office manager.

All groups contribute to one continuing interview. Keep notes in any app you already use, and use quotation marks only for words you recorded accurately. Distinguish the person doing the work from the person approving it. Ask about existing tools and what happened when staff tried earlier changes.

## Compare your notes without AI

1. Identify who is struggling and what they're trying to do.
2. Point to the interview details that support your interpretation.
3. Separate your assumptions from what the office manager described.
4. Name what you still need to confirm.
5. Write a provisional problem statement in your own words.

Groups can interpret the same interview differently when their evidence supports it. Keep unknown costs and targets unknown. A classroom example doesn't establish a measured baseline or confirm requirements for a real implementation.

## Draft the brief together

Read the prompt together, then paste it into Claude Code with your notes:

```text
Read workshop-02/CLAUDE.md and workshop-02/PRD.md for this family-law exercise.
Use superpowers:brainstorming if available, and follow the exercise boundaries.
Use only the interview notes below as case evidence, not the older Meridian case.
Help us clarify the problem from our notes before discussing solutions.
Ask one question at a time to compare possible approaches, including a simpler process change.
After we choose an approach, draft workshop-02/PRD.md for our review.
Do not build the solution. Stop after drafting the PRD and wait for our review.

[Paste the group's interview notes here.]
```

If the skill isn't available, use the same discussion process in the chat. Everyone should help answer Claude's questions and check the draft. The driver operates the computer, but the group decides what the proposal says.

## Review and compare proposals

1. Check each factual claim against your interview notes.
2. Label assumptions and unanswered questions.
3. Explain why you chose this approach over another option.
4. Identify one assumption that could change your recommendation.
5. Save the draft and share its text with your group.

We'll compare two proposals as a class. Explain your reasoning in your own words, and revise anything the discussion shows you need to check. A group decision doesn't establish that the real stakeholder has approved the proposal.

If time remains after review, ask Claude for one `workshop-02/mockup.html` screen or workflow using fictional data. Explain what you want the visual to help you question before asking Claude to create it. A mockup is optional during class.
