## The opening challenge

Suppose your dashboard shows strong sales at Meridian's busiest stores. Dana
says, “Great. That proves Pasadena is the next location.”

Vote: **enough evidence / useful but insufficient / cannot tell yet**.
Write down what the chart would have to tell you before you would commit money.
Then compare your answer with a partner. We will revisit your vote after the interview.

“Sales tells us how much money came in. What else would we need to know to
decide whether another store is a good investment?”

## Interview exercise

Work in groups of four: stakeholder, facilitator, note-taker, and timekeeper.
In groups of three, combine note-taker and timekeeper. Only the stakeholder
opens the [Dana role card](stakeholder-role-card.md) before the interviews.
Everyone else uses the client brief. The card adds fictional facts for this
exercise; discover them through questions rather than reading ahead.

Use one conversation per breakout room. The facilitator leads, the note-taker
records evidence and gets a follow-up question, and the timekeeper protects
the final two minutes for a read-back. Every analyst contributes a question.
Rotate the facilitator in round 2; keep the same Dana for continuity. Each
student writes their own statements and reflection, including the student
playing Dana. Dana's student preserves a first statement before opening the
card and identifies role-card evidence separately from interview evidence.

**Round 1, 25 minutes:** 3 minutes to prepare roles, 15 minutes to interview,
2 minutes to read back, and 5 minutes to compare notes. **Round 2, 20 minutes:**
3 minutes to prioritize gaps, 12 minutes to interview, 2 minutes to read back,
and 3 minutes to capture corrections.

Try questions such as:

- What will the board decide, and what options are still open?
- What happened that made this important now? Can you give an example?
- What would make you advise against Pasadena?
- When you say “performing,” what would count as success? Who approves that?
- What do you need in three weeks, and what can wait until week eight?
- Which evidence can we access, who owns it, and what can we share?

Ask a neutral follow-up before offering your interpretation. “Do you agree
Pasadena is risky?” supplies your answer. “What concerns do you have about
Pasadena?” lets Dana supply hers. A confident answer is still a claim to verify.

At the end, say: “I heard that you need to decide ___ by ___. We can provide
___, but we still need to confirm ___. What have I misunderstood?”

## Use DCACT to keep the decision in view

| Step | Apply it to this engagement |
|---|---|
| D = Define the business problem | Identify the decision, decision owner, business consequence, deadline, and success criteria. This is today's focus. |
| C = Collect and prepare data | Inventory available evidence, missing evidence, owners, and restrictions. Plan acquisition; Workshop 3 develops it. |
| A = Analyze and generate insights | Choose the depth supported by the problem and data: descriptive, diagnostic, predictive, or prescriptive. State where you stop and why. |
| C = Communicate the results | Present findings, uncertainty, and the recommendation appropriate to the board checkpoint. |
| T = Take action and monitor | Identify who acts and how outcomes would be monitored. Do not treat presentation as the final action. |

Descriptive work asks what happened. Diagnostic work investigates why.
Predictive work estimates what might happen. Prescriptive work compares what
to do. You do not need all four. Historical sales alone cannot establish
Pasadena's future profitability, and a possible explanation is not a proven cause.
If evidence supports only a preliminary comparison, stop there and state what
would be needed for the next decision.
